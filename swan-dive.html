What you actually drafted

Three things about this roster are worth knowing before anything else.

Your receivers are the best part of this team, by a long way. Justin Jefferson and Jaxon Smith-Njigba is close to the best pair of wide receivers anybody in your league will put on the field. Smith-Njigba averaged 21 points a game last season, second in the entire NFL, and Jefferson is the most talented receiver of his generation coming off a year that was strange rather than bad. In a league that pays a point for every catch, that is a very good place to be strong.

Your running backs are the part to keep an eye on. Breece Hall and Quinshon Judkins are both real starters, and both have a real question attached — Hall does not score enough touchdowns, and Judkins averaged only 3.6 yards a carry last year. Unlike some teams in your league, you do not own the backup to either of them. If one gets hurt, you are shopping.

And your bench is unusually well spread out. Wan'Dale Robinson is off in Week 9 and Michael Wilson in Week 14, weeks when nobody else on your roster is off at all. That sounds like a small thing. It means they are actually available in every week you might need them, which is more than most benches manage.

There is one week buried in the schedule that needs planning, and it is Week 11. Worth knowing about now rather than the morning it happens.

The thing to plan around

Bye weeks

Every team in the league gets one week off. A player on a bye scores exactly zero, and forgetting is the most common way people lose a week they should have won.

The one that will hurt

Week 11. Seattle and Cleveland are both off, which takes out Jaxon Smith-Njigba, Jason Myers and Quinshon Judkins in one go — your best receiver, your kicker and a starting running back. You can cover the receiver and the running back off your own bench. The kicker you cannot, because you only own one. The two weeks that cost you nothing

Week 9 and Week 14 take out Wan'Dale Robinson and Michael Wilson respectively, and both of them are bench players. A bench player on a bye scores you zero, which is exactly what he was scoring you anyway. Do nothing.

Running the team

When to make a move

Your league uses priority order waivers, not a bidding budget. That changes how you should play it.

How priority works. Everyone in the league is in a queue. When you successfully claim a player, you go to the back of it. So your position is a one-shot resource — spending it is not free, and the question is never "do I want this player" but "is this player worth going to the back of the line for."

The answer is usually no. Spend it on somebody who will actually start for you — a running back who just inherited a starting job, or a receiver whose team's number one just got hurt. Do not spend it on a hunch or a player you would leave on your bench.

Claims process overnight. Put them in Tuesday evening. You do not have to be the fastest, you have to be early enough to be in the batch.

Somebody on your roster gets hurt, or a player nobody owns has a huge afternoon. This is the whole decision — and most weeks it ends in the very first box.

The honest answer is do nothing most weeks, and that is not laziness. An unspent waiver priority is worth far more in Week 10, when somebody's starting running back goes down, than a hunch is worth in Week 3.

The three situations worth spending on

One: a starting running back gets hurt and you can get his backup before anyone else notices — that backup just inherited twenty touches a week. Two: you need a real body for a bye week and there is nothing on your bench, which for you means a kicker in Week 11 and a defence in Week 5. Three: your defence has a genuinely awful run of opponents coming and there is a better one sitting free.

Should you swap your defence?

For your team, more than most: yes, eventually. The standard advice is to “stream” defences — drop yours every week and pick up whoever is playing the worst offence that Sunday. It works because a defence's score depends far more on who they are playing than on how good they are.

Right now, hold the Chiefs. Not because they are good — because their schedule is. Kansas City has the easiest run of opponents in the league through the first four weeks and the third easiest overall start. Ride that for as long as it lasts.

But be honest about them after that. They lost two starting defensive backs and did not really replace them, they were 30th in the league at forcing fumbles, and they scored exactly zero defensive touchdowns all of last season. Turnovers are where fantasy defensive points come from, and they were not generating any. This is not the Chiefs defence people remember.

So the plan is: ride the easy schedule, then reassess in October. You have to drop them in Week 5 for their bye anyway, so you will already have found out how easy defences are to pick up.

One question worth asking your commissioner

In a lot of leagues, once waivers have processed, anyone left unclaimed becomes a free agent you can add instantly without spending your waiver position. Defences and kickers are almost always sitting there unclaimed. If your league works that way, both of your bye-week problems become free — and you could swap defences weekly at no cost. It is one message to find out.

What a good defensive matchup actually looks like

When you do go looking, this is what you want: a defence playing a team with a backup quarterback, or one that turns the ball over a lot, or one with injuries on its offensive line. Home teams are slightly better than away teams. A mediocre defence facing a bad offence beats a famous defence facing a good one, every time.

Trading

A trade is just two managers swapping players, both agreeing, no waiver position spent. Most people in a ten-team league make one or two all season, and plenty make none, so there is no obligation here at all.

There is really only one rule: trade from surplus to fix a hole. Your surplus is at receiver. You start two plus a flex, and you own five — and two of them, Wan'Dale Robinson and Michael Wilson, are good enough to start for somebody else's team while sitting on your bench doing nothing for you.

Your hole is running back certainty. Breece Hall and Quinshon Judkins are your only two real starters at the position, you own neither of their backups, and the rest of your running backs are lottery tickets. One injury and you have a genuine problem.

So the trade to look for is a bench receiver for a running back you would actually start. You are not weakening your starting lineup at all — Jefferson, Smith-Njigba and Egbuka do not move — and you are turning a player who scores you nothing into one who scores you something every week.

How to make an offer somebody accepts. Say what you want and why it helps them, in one message. Do not lowball, do not send five offers in a day, and expect most of them to be declined. That is normal and it is not personal. The trade deadline is usually around Week 11, so there is no rush.

And do not trade in a mood. The two worst moments to make a deal are straight after a bad week, when you want to do something, and straight after a good one, when you feel untouchable.

The ninety-second routine

Sunday morning, before the games: open the app, check nobody is injured or on a bye, and set your lineup. Each player locks at his own kickoff, not all at once — so somebody playing in the late afternoon can still be swapped at two o'clock. More people lose weeks by forgetting to look than by making bad decisions.

And at the end of the day

You have two Seahawks, and Seattle just won the Super Bowl, so Jaxon Smith-Njigba and Jason Myers will be on television more than anyone else you own. You have a Chiefs defence you are now obliged to cheer for, which takes some getting used to. And you have Kenny Gainwell, who spent last season in Pittsburgh and led the Steelers in catches before leaving for Tampa Bay — the one black-and-gold thread running through this whole roster.

But none of this is the point. Fantasy football is a season-long side quest — a reason to care about a Cleveland game at one o'clock and to know what a good afternoon looks like in numbers. The actual thing, the thing that will still be going in twenty years, is the black and gold on a Sunday with the towel in your hand.

Win your league. Then go beat Baltimore.
