The Steelers Playbook

A Sunday this autumn · fourth quarter

The game is close. Pittsburgh's defense needs one stop.

The stadium lights drop. A guitar riff comes over the speakers, and sixty-five thousand people stand up at once and start waving gold towels above their heads.

The noise gets loud enough that eleven grown men twenty yards away cannot hear each other speak, and the other team has to stop the game and waste a timeout.

That's “Renegade,” and it happens several times a season. It is the single best ninety seconds in American sport, and the first time you see it you will understand this entire city instantly. Everything in this guide exists so that when it happens, you know exactly what's at stake and why everyone is standing.

Chapter 01 — The atom

What one play is

Start here. Almost everyone who finds football confusing was never told this part, and nothing else makes sense without it.

Football is not a flow like soccer or basketball. It happens in short, separate bursts. Here is one of them.

Everything stops. Both teams walk up and line up facing each other along an invisible line running across the field — the line of scrimmage — with the ball on the ground between them. Nobody may cross it yet.

One player on the team with the ball, the center, has his hands on it. He flings it backward between his legs to the quarterback standing behind him. That backward toss is called the snap, and it is the starting gun — everyone is allowed to move the instant it happens.

For the next four to seven seconds, all twenty-two people move at once. Either someone runs with the ball, or the quarterback throws it to someone. A whistle blows the moment the ball carrier is tackled to the ground, runs out of bounds, or a thrown ball hits the turf uncaught.

That's one play. Then everyone gets up, walks back, and lines up again on a new line of scrimmage — wherever the ball ended up. And they do it again. About 130 times in a game.

The sentence to hang on to

Football is a series of arguments about one line — and every few seconds, the line moves. Whoever moves it forward is winning.

The team with the ball is the offense. The team trying to stop them is the defense. Every club has both, and they swap out completely: when Pittsburgh has the ball, eleven Steelers offensive players are on the field, and the moment Pittsburgh loses it, those eleven jog off and eleven different Steelers run on.

This is the first thing that throws people. It looks like the team keeps fielding entirely different players. It does. They're specialists. A defensive lineman has never caught a pass in his life and doesn't want to.

Chapter 02 — The engine

Four tries to go ten yards

This is the rule the entire sport is built on. Learn it and most of what commentators say stops being noise.

The offense doesn't get to keep running plays forever. They get four plays — each one is called a down — to move the ball ten yards forward.

Make the ten yards, and the count resets: four fresh downs, a new ten yards to chase. That reset is a first down, and it's why a crowd will erupt over a three-yard play that looked like nothing.

Fail to make it in four, and you hand the ball to the other team right where you stopped — usually terrible field position for you. So on fourth down teams almost always punt instead: kick it far downfield, giving up the ball on purpose so at least the opponent starts a long way from your end zone.

So 2nd and 7 means: second of the four tries, seven yards still needed. 3rd and 1 is nearly a sure thing. 3rd and 15 means the last two plays went badly.

If it helps

Think of it as a credit card with a ten-yard minimum payment. Make the minimum and your limit resets and you keep spending. Miss it four times and the card is taken away, wherever you happen to be standing.

Chapter 03 — The arithmetic

Points, and the shape of a season

Five ways to score, and one map of how a whole year of this fits together.

Touchdown — 6 points. Get the ball into the end zone, carrying it in or catching it in there. The main event.

Extra point — 1. A short kick straight after a touchdown. Nearly automatic, which is why 7 is the most common single score.

Two-point conversion — 2. Instead of that kick, run one more play from close range. Riskier, worth double, used when a team needs one exact number.

Field goal — 3. Kick it through the tall yellow posts from anywhere. The consolation prize when a drive stalls.

Safety — 2. The rare one: the defense tackles the ball carrier inside his own end zone. The defending team gets the points and the ball. It happens a handful of times a season and the building comes apart.

Why scores look the way they do

Because a touchdown-plus-kick is 7 and a field goal is 3, nearly every football score is built out of 7s and 3s. That's what people mean by “it's a two-score game” — being down 10 needs a touchdown and a field goal, which is a completely different situation from being down 7, even though the numbers look close.

How a season is shaped

Thirty-two teams, split into two halves called conferences — the AFC and the NFC. Pittsburgh is in the AFC. Each conference is cut into four divisions of four teams. Pittsburgh's is the AFC North: the Steelers, the Baltimore Ravens, the Cleveland Browns, and the Cincinnati Bengals. You play those three rivals twice each, every single year, which is exactly why the hatred is so specific.

Everyone plays 17 games across 18 weeks — the spare week is your bye, a scheduled week off, and every team gets one. Best records make the playoffs, a single-elimination bracket: lose once and your season is finished. The last AFC team standing plays the last NFC team standing in the Super Bowl.

Regular season runs September to early January. Playoffs through January. Super Bowl in February. Then everyone talks about the draft for six months.

The shortcut, before anything else

Only four positions ever really touch the ball: quarterback, running back, wide receiver, tight end. Those are the names you'll hear all game, and — not by coincidence — they're the only ones you draft in fantasy. Learn those four and you can follow any broadcast tonight. Everything else is detail you can pick up over a season.

The Pittsburgh-specific thing to know

Most teams line up four big men on the defensive line. Pittsburgh has spent forty years running a 3–4 instead — only three linemen, with four linebackers behind them. The two on the outside are edge rushers, and their whole job is coming around the side to flatten the quarterback. Nearly every terrifying Steelers defender you'll hear named — Greg Lloyd, Joey Porter, James Harrison, T.J. Watt — played that exact position. Chapter 05 — In the room

How to actually watch

The rhythm, the interruptions, and what everyone is reacting to.

Four quarters, fifteen minutes each, with halftime in the middle. The clock stops constantly — on incomplete passes, when a player goes out of bounds, after scores — which is why sixty minutes of football takes three hours of your afternoon.

The last two minutes are a different sport. Late in a close game the clock becomes the opponent: the team behind throws short passes out of bounds to stop it, the team ahead runs the ball to burn it. If it's close at the two-minute warning, put your phone down.

Turnovers are the biggest swing in football. An interception (a defender catches a pass) or a fumble (the carrier drops it and the other team lands on it) flips possession instantly. Every fumble recovery in history is somebody guessing which way an oval bounces.

A yellow flag on the grass is a penalty. Somebody broke a rule; the referee announces it and moves the ball five, ten, or fifteen yards. Holding and pass interference are the two you'll see most, and the two that get shouted about most.

Two questions that make you good to watch with

“Why did they go for it there?” and “Was that a good throw or a bad drop?” Nobody in the room knows the answer with certainty, which is exactly why they'll happily argue about it for ten minutes. Asking beats reciting.

Chapter 06 — The reason we're here

Why Pittsburgh is different

Thirty-two teams play this sport. This one has a personality, and it's worth knowing before you're in a room full of people who assume you already do.

Pittsburgh was a steel town, and the football team has never once let anyone forget it. Black and gold. The logo sits on one side of the helmet only, because it was put there as a trial in the sixties, the team immediately started winning, and nobody has dared touch it since.

What that adds up to in practice, for the games you're about to watch: this team is expected to play hard defense, run the ball, and win ugly. When Pittsburgh wins 17–13 in the rain, that is not a bad game — that is the game working exactly as intended, and the room will be delighted.

The rivalry that matters

The Baltimore Ravens. Same division, twice a year, always physical, usually decided by a field goal, and genuinely unpleasant in a way the other games aren't. Cleveland and Cincinnati are the other two division rivals and they matter, but Baltimore is the one. If you only pay real attention to two games a year, make it those.

Chapter 07 — Your league

Fantasy football, properly

You aren't picking teams. You're running a roster of real players from all over the league, and their real statistics become your points.

Before the season you and roughly ten or twelve other people take turns picking real NFL players — that's the draft. Each week you choose which of your players to start. What those players actually do on Sunday converts into points, and your total is compared head-to-head against one other person in your league. Higher score wins. Do that for about fourteen weeks and the best records make your league's playoffs.

The genuinely fun part: it makes every game matter. You will find yourself shouting at a Jacksonville game at one in the afternoon because your running back needs six more yards. This is normal. This is the point.

Draft day, mechanically

It's a snake draft. The order reverses every round. If you pick 3rd of 12, you pick 3rd, then 22nd, then 27th, then 46th. So an early pick means a long wait for your second one, and if you're picking near the end you get two in quick succession — plan them as a pair.

There's a clock. Usually 60–90 seconds per pick. If it runs out, the app drafts for you, and it will draft badly. Use the queue: while other people are picking, line up the next three players you want, so you're never deciding from scratch.

Learn one piece of jargon: ADP. Average Draft Position — roughly where each player normally goes. Your app shows it next to every name. If someone available has an ADP of 20 and you're at pick 45, that's a bargain. It's the single most useful number on the screen and it stops you taking someone forty picks too early.

Do two mock drafts first. Free, twenty minutes each, inside whichever app your league uses. Every mechanical surprise happens there instead of in front of your friends. This is the highest-value twenty minutes in this entire guide.

Ask this before you draft

“Is it PPR, and is it superflex?” PPR means a point per catch, which makes receivers and pass-catching running backs far more valuable. Superflex means you can start a second quarterback — and in that format the advice below about waiting on quarterbacks is completely wrong, and you should take one early. Both settings are in your league's rules page. Two minutes of checking.

The in-season routine

Tuesday night

Put in your waiver claims for players you want. Claims process overnight in a single batch — usually early Wednesday — and resolve in priority order. You don't have to be fastest, you have to be early. This is where leagues are won.

Sunday morning

Check the injury report and set your lineup. Each player locks at his own kickoff, not all at once — so a 4:25 player can still be swapped at 2pm. Never leave a questionable player in and forget: an inactive scores zero.

Every single week

Actually open the app. More fantasy losses come from lineups nobody set — a player on a bye scoring zero — than from bad drafting. It takes ninety seconds.

When someone gets hurt

His backup just inherited twenty touches a game. Claim him immediately, even if your bench is full — drop your worst player without agonising. Whoever claims him instead will beat you with him in November.

Trades

Reply to every offer, even to decline. Be sceptical of two-for-one deals: you can only start nine players, so giving up depth means someone off your bench now starts, and they're on your bench for a reason.

The one real faux pas

Don't stop setting your lineup in Week 13 because you're eliminated. A dead team wrecks other people's playoff races, and it's the only thing that genuinely annoys a league.

Chapter 09 — The payoff

Your first Sunday

Everything above was so that this afternoon makes sense.

When it happens

Most games kick off 1:00pm and 4:25pm Eastern on Sunday, with one more Sunday night. There's also a Monday night game and a Thursday night game each week. Pittsburgh's slot moves around — check on Tuesday.

Get NFL RedZone

A single channel that jumps between every game in progress and shows you every scoring play, live, with no adverts. For someone with fantasy players scattered across eight teams it is genuinely the best thing on television. Seven hours, no breaks.

Watch for “Renegade”

Fourth quarter, close game, Steelers defense needs a stop. They cut the lights, play a specific Styx song, and sixty-five thousand people wave gold towels at once. Opposing offenses have burned timeouts because they couldn't hear each other. It's the best ninety seconds in football — ask for it by name.

What to actually watch

Not the ball. Watch the yellow line and ask, every play, whether they got past it. That single habit is the difference between watching twenty-two men collide and watching a game.

And the towel

You wave it over your head, in a circle — on third downs, after big plays, and continuously through any tight fourth quarter. It was invented in 1975 by a radio broadcaster named Myron Cope, whose station wanted a gimmick. He later signed its rights over to a school for people with intellectual disabilities, where his son lived, and it has raised millions since. It's been to the summit of Everest and aboard the Space Station. Pittsburgh is not subtle about this team.
