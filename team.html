Running the team

When to make a move

Your league uses priority order waivers, not a bidding budget. That changes how you should play it.

How priority works. Everyone in the league is in a queue. When you successfully claim a player, you go to the back of it. So your position is a one-shot resource — spending it is not free, and the question is never "do I want this player" but "is this player worth going to the back of the line for."

The answer is usually no. Spend it on somebody who will actually start for you — a running back who just inherited a starting job, or a receiver whose team's number one just got hurt. Do not spend it on a hunch or a player you would leave on your bench.

Claims process overnight. Put them in Tuesday evening. You do not have to be the fastest, you have to be early enough to be in the batch.

The three situations worth spending on

One: a starting running back gets hurt and you can get his backup before anyone else notices — that backup just inherited twenty touches a week. Two: you need a real body for a bye week and there is nothing on your bench. Three: your defense has a genuinely awful run of opponents coming and there is a better one sitting free, which happens more than you'd think.

Should you swap your defense every week?

You will hear this advice constantly, and it is worth understanding because it is usually right and for your league mostly isn't.

The standard advice is to “stream” defenses — drop yours every week and pick up whichever one is playing the worst offense that Sunday. It works because a defense's score depends far more on who they are playing than on how good they are. A mediocre defense facing a bad quarterback will usually outscore a great defense facing a good one.

But streaming costs you something in your league. You are on priority-order waivers, so every claim sends you to the back of the queue. A defense is the lowest-scoring slot on your roster — the gap between a good week and a bad one is maybe five points. The claim that actually decides a season is the running back who inherits a starting job in Week 6 when somebody gets hurt, and you need your priority intact for that. Spending it on a five-point upgrade at your smallest position is a bad trade.

So the answer for you is: keep the Eagles. They are genuinely good rather than merely adequate — Vic Fangio has a long reputation as one of the best defensive coaches in football, and they finished eighth in the league in pressure rate, which is the thing that actually generates sacks and turnovers. A good defense held all season beats a middling one streamed badly.

One question worth asking your commissioner

In a lot of leagues, once waivers have processed, anyone left unclaimed becomes a free agent you can add instantly without spending your waiver position. Defenses are almost always sitting there unclaimed. If your league works that way, streaming is nearly free and this whole calculation flips — you could swap defenses weekly at no cost. It is one message to find out, and it is worth sending.

The three times to move off the Eagles anyway

Week 10, without fail. That is their bye and they score zero, so you need somebody in that slot regardless of what it costs. If they stop being good — key injuries, or a run of genuinely bad performances rather than one quiet week. Or if they hit a brutal stretch of opponents with strong offenses, which you can see coming a few weeks out.

What a good defensive matchup actually looks like

If you ever do go looking, this is what you want: a defense playing a team with a backup quarterback, or one that turns the ball over a lot, or one with injuries on its offensive line. Home teams are slightly better than away teams. And a defense playing an offense that struggles to score is worth far more than a famous defense playing a good one — the opponent matters more than the name.

Trading

A trade is just two managers swapping players, both agreeing, no waiver position spent. Most people in a ten-team league make one or two all season, and plenty make none, so there is no obligation here at all.

There is really only one rule: trade from surplus to fix a hole. You start two running backs and a flex, and you own five running backs. Three of them cannot play for you in a normal week. That is surplus, and surplus sitting on a bench is worth nothing — a bench player scores you exactly zero points.

But not all of your surplus is spare. Brian Robinson Jr. and Mike Washington Jr. are insurance on Bijan and Jeanty, and insurance is worth keeping. Jonathon Brooks is the one who is genuinely spare — he backs up nobody you own. If you ever trade anyone, it should probably be him.

How to make an offer somebody accepts. Say what you want and why it helps them, in one message: “You are thin at running back and I have four. Brooks for one of your receivers?” Do not lowball, do not send five offers in a day, and expect most of them to be declined. That is normal and it is not personal.

And do not trade in a mood. The two worst moments to make a deal are straight after a bad week, when you want to do something, and straight after a good one, when you feel untouchable. The trade deadline is usually around Week 11, so there is no rush.
